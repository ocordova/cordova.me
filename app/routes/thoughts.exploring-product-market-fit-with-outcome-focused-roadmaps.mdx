Most roadmaps are lists of tasks with deadlines. The team ships each item, checks it off, and moves on. The problem is that finishing tasks doesn't mean you're any closer to product-market fit.

An outcomes-driven roadmap changes the unit of work. Instead of "Develop notification system," the goal is "Increase user engagement by 25% by the end of Q4." Instead of "Send promotional email campaign," it's "Increase sales by 8%." The task becomes a means to an end, and the team gets room to figure out the best way to get there.

The metric matters as much as the goal. We use the [HEART framework](https://www.productplan.com/glossary/heart-framework/) to choose ours: Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, and Task Success. It keeps us from picking a number just because it looks good.

This only works if the team believes the goal. A roadmap full of outcomes that nobody checks is just a prettier todo list. But when the outcome is real, the conversation changes. People stop asking "Did we ship it?" and start asking "Did it move the number?"

Product-market fit is the point where those numbers start moving on their own. A good roadmap doesn't guarantee you'll get there, but a bad one almost guarantees you won't.
